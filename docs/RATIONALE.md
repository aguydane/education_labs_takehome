# Helm — design rationale (draft)

> Draft assembled overnight from the design conversation, for Dane to rewrite in his own voice. Everything here traces to a decision made in that conversation; nothing should survive into the submission that he wouldn't say himself. Sections follow the assignment's list.

## Which option, and why

Option B: learning through collaboration with Claude.

The problem I wanted to work on is one I feel directly. Working with Claude makes me enormously productive, and that productivity creates pressure: there is always something I could be shipping, so learning starts to feel like a cost. But the skills I already have are what let me steer. They give me richer, more precise intentions, a sharper eye for whether an output is going in the direction I meant, and the imagination to cross into adjacent territory. When that capacity grows, and when I feel safe spending time on it, the work with AI gets better, not slower.

So Helm is not about doing the work by hand instead of delegating. Claude and the models after it will apply most skills faster and more capaciously than I will. It is about **steering capacity**: understanding deep enough to specify, judge, and redirect. Claude does the work; you keep the understanding.

## The design

Four steps, run continuously alongside ordinary work.

**Harvest.** After every exchange, a background call asks: what conceptual knowledge would a person need to understand this exchange and its output well enough to have produced it, given time? And how confident can we be, from the learner's own words, that they have it? It never interrupts. Everything it records is visible on chips under each message, and "keep delegating this" is one click, because not everything is worth understanding.

**Prune.** Claude proposes a small active set with plain-language reasoning; the learner decides, on a knowledge graph that is the primary learning surface rather than a decoration. The active set is sticky by design. Slots turn over when a concept graduates or the learner drops it, never because something newer came along. Interest-based exploration lives on the graph as adjacent nodes and a "curiosity pin" that protects a concept from the ranking.

**Practice.** Two modes, because in my own experience these are two different things. *Beat* is a two-to-five-minute aside inside work, triggered by the learner's own bid ("why did you do it that way?") or at a pause point. *Studio* is dedicated, protected, calendar-blocked, state-saved time: your calendar shows the block, your colleagues see Busy, your work is summarized and saved so it's safe to leave. Studio coaches on the learner's own past exchange, on a rung the learner can move: modeling (Claude does it and narrates), coaching (you do it by hand, Claude asks before it tells), fading (Claude is available, not present). Doing it by hand is the method; steering evidence is the measure.

**Recognize.** Later, when the learner's own prompts, critiques, or session structure show a concept in use ("two weeks ago you asked me to make this query faster; today you asked for a partial index and a planner check"), Claude proposes a recognition, quoting their words. The learner confirms or rejects, and every rejection routes somewhere: "I copied a pattern" is the strongest possible harvest signal; "I already knew this" corrects the model; "that's not the concept" fixes the rubric.

The best trigger turned out to be one I know from my own days: the moment after kicking off long-horizon agent work. There is nothing to produce, the safety framing is airtight because you'd be waiting anyway, and the most situated material possible is whatever the agent is doing right now.

## Process

Started from the feeling (productivity pressure crowds out learning), then argued about what learning is *for* when the AI will do the work anyway. That reframe from "owning skills" to "steering capacity" changed all four steps. Then pruned hard: time tracking became a single budget number, a scheduler became a calendar strip, a memory system became a JSON blob behind a storage interface. Wrote a build plan before code and argued with it. Built the pipeline and prompts first, UI second, with the seed data generated by running the real pipeline over synthetic transcripts rather than hand-writing what the system "would" produce.

## How the design enhances rather than replaces human agency

One rule everywhere: **Claude observes and proposes; the learner judges.** Harvest is legible and correctable. Prune never auto-swaps. Recognition is proposed, never asserted, and the learner's rejection reasons are treated as information. The mode switch is explicit and always one click away. The Studio rung is the learner's to move. Even the seeds respect this: the active set in each seed was chosen by the pipeline's proposal, but a real learner would have been the one to accept it.

## Learning principles

- **Self-determination theory.** Autonomy (the learner picks), competence (visible, informational evidence of progress), and the finding that surveillance and controlling rewards undermine intrinsic motivation. Helm's harvest *is* surveillance, so it has to be legibly for the learner and under their control; recognition is phrased as evidence, never as a reward.
- **Scaffolding with fading.** The rung ladder is the explicit fading schedule scaffolding research says is usually missing. Modeling first for a first encounter; fading once there's evidence. The expertise-reversal effect is why "let me try" exists.
- **Situated learning.** Practice on the learner's own exchanges, not textbook examples. The long-task trigger is the most situated moment available. Studio's decontextualization is mitigated by always working on real material. The honest limitation: Helm is a dyad; there's no community of practice.
- **Retrieval, spacing, and the generation effect.** Recognition is retrieval in the wild. Graduation requires confirmations spread over time. By-hand practice in Studio is generation.
- **Interleaving vs. blocked practice.** Two or three active concepts, held for weeks, with an exploration side channel that doesn't compete for the slots.

## Measuring success

Not productivity metrics. Leading indicators: recognitions confirmed per week and the ratio of confirmed to rejected (precision of the learner model); the share of Beats taken when offered (are triggers landing at the right moments?); Studio sessions completed with a closing statement; and the distance between the language of a learner's early and late prompts on an active concept. Lagging: concepts graduating to durable; learners' own reports of whether they felt safe spending the time.

## Scaling

The pipeline is pure functions over a transcript store behind a storage interface. In production, one row per learner, fed by every Claude surface (Claude.ai, Claude Code, plugins via hooks), with harvest and recognize running online per exchange against a compact projection of the learner model, and decay, graduation, consolidation and prune running nightly through the Batch API. Per-exchange cost is bounded by the exchange and the concept index, not by history. Surfaces have two jobs: post exchanges, and render offers and recognitions while accepting judgments. Section 3.1 of the build plan walks through session N+1.
